Tenant Impact and Ethics

Tenant views on the responsible use of AI

Tenant & EthicsResearch reportWorkstream 4 · October 2026

Combined findings from Awen and Incommunities customer engagement

Workstream 4: Tenant impact and ethics | October 2026

About this report

This publication brings together survey and roundtable evidence from 174 customer participants: 50 Awen customers and 124 Incommunities customers concerning the use of AI by landlords. It is intended to provide helpful sector learning and lead to further actions that landlords can undertake alongside their customers to provide reassurance around the use of AI, as well as ensuring effective collaboration in the development of use cases and service improvements that flow from increasing AI adoption in the sector.

1 Executive summary

HAILIE Workstream 4 explored how tenants view social landlords’ use of artificial intelligence and what responsible, transparent adoption should look like. Across the engagement, views are mixed but highly consistent on the conditions that matter.

Strongest overall message

Tenants are not simply asking whether landlords should use AI. They want to know where it is used, why it is used, what information it relies on, who checks it, and how they can reach a person or challenge an outcome.

  • Human contact must remain available, especially when an issue is complex, sensitive or urgent.
  • AI should assist colleagues rather than replace human responsibility for important decisions.
  • People expect clear explanations when AI is used, what it can and cannot do, and what difference it makes.
  • Privacy, security, accuracy and fairness are central to trust.
  • Customers need straightforward routes to ask questions, correct information, request review and complain.
  • Accessible and non-digital alternatives are essential for people who face digital or other barriers.
  • Acceptance depends on the task and potential impact. Lower-risk assistance attracts more support than customer-facing or consequential decision-making.
  • Tenants want to remain involved as uses develop and evidence changes.

The combined evidence supports a careful, staged and accountable model of adoption. Technology should improve outcomes and staff capacity without weakening choice, rights, accessibility or the human relationship.

2 About the research

This report has been produced through HAILIE’s Workstream 4 activity on tenant impact and ethics. It combines customer research undertaken by Awen and Incommunities to create a stronger shared evidence base for the housing sector.

OrganisationEngagementParticipantsContribution to the evidence
AwenAI transparency survey and tenant digital roundtable50Structured and qualitative insight on understanding, privacy, human involvement, fairness, challenge, inclusion and ongoing tenant involvement.
IncommunitiesAI transparency survey with closed, multiple-choice and free-text questions124Quantitative and qualitative evidence on sentiment, comfort with different uses, concerns, trust, transparency and human review.

The report treats the findings as one body of sector insight. The different methods add breadth and depth, but the findings are not combined into a single score or used to compare the participating organisations.

Research principle

The findings are directional rather than statistically representative. Their value lies in the consistency of the expectations expressed across two organisations and different engagement methods.

3 What tenants told us

1

Keep a human route

Tenants want a clear and straightforward way to speak to a person, particularly where circumstances are personal, urgent, complex or not understood by an automated service.

2

Keep people responsible

AI may support colleagues, but landlords should remain accountable. Important outputs and decisions must be capable of human checking, escalation and correction.

3

Be open in practice

People want to be told when and why AI is used, what it is helping with, what it cannot do, what information is involved and who remains responsible.

4

Protect information

Concerns include confidentiality, scams, third-party access, storage, sharing and the security of personal data. Broad assurances are not enough.

5

Build in fairness and accuracy

Tenants expect current and accurate information, checks for bias and unfair outcomes, and action when errors or unintended barriers are found.

6

Make challenge meaningful

People want to ask questions, correct information, request human review, escalate an issue and complain without unnecessary difficulty.

7

Design for inclusion

Digital confidence, age, disability, language, literacy and individual support needs can affect access. Human and non-digital alternatives should remain available.

8

Show the benefit and learn

Efficiency alone is not a sufficient justification. Landlords should demonstrate improved quality, accessibility, consistency and tenant outcomes, and involve tenants in review.

4 How views change by use and impact

The evidence does not support a simple “for or against AI” conclusion. Views change according to the purpose, the information used, the effect on the individual and whether meaningful human support remains available.

More likely to attract conditional supportCreates greater concern
Helping staff summarise routine information or reduce administrative effort.A default approach to replacing direct human contact with an automated interaction.
Finding patterns in feedback or service data to identify emerging issues.Influencing sensitive activity involving vulnerability, support needs or personal circumstances.
Supporting repairs or service planning where staff remain involved.Making, or appearing to make, a final decision without human review.
Helping people navigate information, including literacy or digital support.Using personal information without clarity about purpose, access, storage, sharing and protection.
Answering simple questions where escalation to a person is easy.Introducing AI mainly to reduce staffing rather than improve tenant outcomes.

Proportionate safeguard principle

The greater the potential effect on a tenant’s home, finances, support, rights or access to services, the stronger the explanation, assessment, human review and route to challenge should be.

5 What the combined evidence shows

Taken as a whole, the survey and roundtable evidence points to a consistent set of expectations for responsible use.

FindingWhat the feedback indicates
Support is conditionalPeople can see potential benefits, but acceptance depends on a clear purpose, visible safeguards and evidence that services improve.
Human contact is the strongest shared expectationPeople want an easy route to a person, particularly when circumstances are complex, sensitive, urgent or not understood by an automated service.
Lower-risk assistance is more acceptableUsing AI to support colleagues, analyse themes or simplify routine activity attracts more confidence than customer-facing or consequential decision-making.
Important decisions require human oversightTenants expect staff to check significant outputs, remain accountable and be able to intervene, correct and review.
Transparency must be practicalPeople want to know when and why AI is used, what information is involved, what it can and cannot do, and who remains responsible.
Privacy, accuracy and fairness shape trustConfidence depends on protecting personal information, checking outputs and acting when errors, bias or unintended barriers are identified.
Challenge and correction must be straightforwardPeople expect to ask questions, correct information, request human review, escalate an issue and complain without unnecessary difficulty.
Inclusion must be designed inAccessible, supported and non-digital alternatives are needed for people who face barriers or do not want to use an AI-enabled route.

Across the feedback, support is conditional, human contact and checking matter, and confidence depends on practical transparency and safeguards.

6 A Practical Transparency standard

Transparency requirementWhat it should mean in practice
Tell people when AI is usedUse plain language and do not expect people to infer that AI contributed to a response, recommendation or process.
Explain the purposeDescribe the task and service benefit being sought, rather than presenting efficiency as sufficient justification.
Explain the information involvedState what personal information is used, who can access it, whether it is shared and how it is protected.
Describe human involvementExplain who checks outputs, when review is mandatory and who remains accountable.
Protect the human routeMake it easy to speak to a person or use an appropriate alternative channel without disadvantage.
Enable challenge and correctionExplain how to ask a question, correct information, request review, escalate an issue or complain.
Design for accessibilityProvide suitable accessible and non-digital information and support.
Review and reportMonitor outcomes, involve tenants, publish learning and update information as uses change.

7 Implications for housing providers

PrincipleSector implication
Assist people, do not remove themPrioritise uses that help colleagues and improve routine processes while preserving human access and responsibility.
Match safeguards to impactApply stronger assessment, approval and review to uses affecting homes, finances, support, rights or service access.
Start with defined use casesDocument the task, data, benefit, limitations, owner, checking and escalation route before implementation.
Test before scalingUse pilots and tenant testing, including people likely to encounter accessibility or digital barriers.
Make assurance visibleMaintain clear ownership, audit, incident response and review arrangements, and explain them simply.
Measure outcomes, not only efficiencyAssess quality, accessibility, consistency, complaints, challenges and tenant experience alongside speed or cost.
Keep transparency liveUpdate public information as technology, evidence and uses develop.

8 Recommendations for the sector

RecommendationPurpose
Adopt a human-available standardGuarantee a route to a person for complex, sensitive or unsuccessful automated interactions.
Publish a tenant transparency statementSet out common commitments in plain English and make the document available for local tailoring.
Maintain an AI use-case registerRecord purpose, data, owner, risk, human involvement, supplier, review and public explanation.
Define mandatory human reviewSpecify decisions and circumstances in which staff review and approval cannot be removed.
Assess equality, privacy, security and service riskComplete proportionate assessment before use and revisit it when the system, data or purpose changes.
Provide opt-out and escalation routesOffer suitable alternatives and ensure people are not disadvantaged for requesting human support.
Test with tenants before wider adoptionInclude people with lived experience of disability, vulnerability, language, literacy and digital exclusion.
Train colleaguesCover verification, bias, data handling, accountability, accessible communication and escalation.
Monitor and publish learningTrack accuracy, errors, complaints, challenges, accessibility and outcomes, and explain changes made.
Address workforce and environmental concernsBe open about the intended role of colleagues and consider proportionality and sustainability in procurement and use.

9 Questions for further tenant involvement

  • Which current or proposed uses should be included in public transparency information?
  • Where must human review be mandatory, and who should remain accountable?
  • What information should be provided at the point of an AI-enabled interaction?
  • How should people reach a person, correct information or request review?
  • What accessibility, equality, privacy, security and service assessments should tenants expect?
  • How should providers demonstrate that AI has improved outcomes rather than simply reduced handling time?
  • What role should tenants play in testing, assurance, governance and future review?
  • How often should a transparency statement and use-case information be reviewed?

10 Limitations and Conclusions

Limitations

  • The findings reflect participants who chose to take part and are not statistically representative of all tenants.
  • The two organisations used different engagement methods, so the evidence should not be combined into a single score or comparative ranking.
  • The engagement methods produced different forms of evidence, so the findings should not be interpreted as a single combined score or comparative measure.
  • People may interpret “AI” differently, and views may change when a specific use case, supplier, benefit and safeguards are explained.
  • The findings identify tenant expectations. They do not, by themselves, confirm that any proposed commitment is already organisational policy or operational practice.

Conclusion

Across the engagement, tenants do not reject the potential value of AI. They can see opportunities to improve information, planning, consistency and staff capacity. However, acceptance cannot be assumed and efficiency is not enough.

The conditions for responsible use are clear and consistent: be open, protect personal information, retain human responsibility, preserve routes to a person, design for inclusion, make challenge possible and show that outcomes have improved.

Closing message

Responsible AI in housing will be judged less by the novelty of the technology and more by whether tenants experience choice, safety, fairness, clarity, accountability and meaningful human support.
