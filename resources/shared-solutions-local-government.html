From Shared Problems to Shared Solutions: Lessons from Local Government

Open Source Collaboration Writeup Video November 2026

Will Callaghan, product lead for LocalGov Drupal at the Open Digital Cooperative, on how councils build and buy software together, what it saves, and how social housing could do the same.

Watch the recording View the slides

"We don't need to solve the same problem 317 times." England has 317 councils and around 1,500 registered social housing providers, and many of them pay separately to solve identical problems. Will Callaghan shared two working models from local government, one for building software together and one for buying it together, and the habits that make collaboration last.

Key takeaways
  • Most needs are not unique. "Every council thinks its needs are unique. Most of them aren't." Branding, some content and local integrations differ. Content formats, forms, common integrations and data standards do not.
  • Build together or buy together. LocalGov Drupal shows how to co-build shared software. SELMS shows how to pool demand and buy as one customer.
  • Collaboration pays. LocalGov Drupal councils report 30–50% lower build costs and launches 6–12 months faster. SELMS reports £81k saved per year.
  • Make it someone's job. Shared projects fail when nobody owns them. Both models fund dedicated people to keep things moving.
  • Start small and grow. LocalGov Drupal began with two councils and a handshake, and now has 80+.

1. The problem and the opportunity

Will opened with a question for the room: "How many of you are currently paying for a system that someone else in this room is also paying for?" Councils, like housing providers, often scope, specify and procure the same thing in parallel. Each pays the full cost, and each has little leverage with suppliers.

Unique to each organisation

Keep local

Branding, some content, bespoke integrations and location-specific data.

Shared with everyone

Solve once

Content formats, forms, common integrations and data standards.

When two or three organisations describe the same need, that is the signal to prioritise it.

3. LocalGov Drupal: building together

LocalGov Drupal

80+ councils

"80+ councils co-design and build together."

How it works

  • A set of open-source website modules maintained by the Open Digital Cooperative, a limited company
  • Co-funded by annual subscriptions from councils and suppliers
  • Councils pick the modules they want and host them how they like
  • The core team encourages co-design and contributions

What it fixes

  • Shared work being no one's job
  • Misaligned incentives
  • The upfront effort each council would face alone

How it grew

  • 2 councils: occasional meetings and emails, a handshake agreement
  • 8 councils: regular group meetings, a light-touch contributor agreement
  • 80+ councils: groups and asynchronous work, a multi-stakeholder co-op

Takeaway: it started at Brighton and Hove Council in 2017–18 and now covers about a third of the council website market. Governance grew with the community, not ahead of it.

Paying for the day-to-day

LocalGov Drupal charges annual subscriptions to councils and suppliers using a tiered fee structure pioneered by the Co-operative Councils' Innovation Network (CCIN). Everyone pays a base fee of £750, then a percentage based on population and budget. The minimum is £1,200 and the maximum £9,750. Will said this brings in around £350,000 a year.

Crowdfunding major features

Bigger pieces of work are funded Kickstarter-style. Councils and suppliers suggest projects, which are specified and costed. Work starts when enough money has been pledged. The biggest so far:

  • Committee management system integration (Quorum CMIS), funded by many councils: £80k
  • PDF importer (Southwark): £25k
  • Elections (Cumberland and Walsall): £15k
  • Consultations (Walsall): £10k

4. SELMS: buying together

South East Library Management System (SELMS)

14 library services

"14 libraries unpack common needs, then buy together."

How it works

  • Terms set by a memorandum of agreement
  • Members chip in for two staff, employed by one of the councils
  • Upgrades are easy to buy through an existing framework
  • The vendor, Civica, likes it too

What it fixes

  • Fragmented demand
  • Slow procurement
  • Misaligned incentives and poor technology
  • Possibly opens the door to new entrants

Results

  • "£81k saved per year"
  • "Costs cut by 50%"
  • Consortium-wide negotiations, for example with eMagazine providers

Takeaway: you do not need to write code to collaborate. Pooling demand gives buyers more clout than any one of them has alone.

Similar models

  • Strata is delivering Open Revenues and Benefits for its three councils, and potentially the wider sector.
  • Veritau Public Sector runs legal, audit and assurance services for its six councils.
  • Advance Market Commitments guarantee the first customers for new public sector software, which can encourage innovation and new entrants. DSIT used one last year.

5. Five habits worth adopting

  1. Start with common needsPick something you are already buying.
  2. Have just enough governanceIdeally it should feel "fun".
  3. Make it someone's jobCould you crowdfund a role?
  4. Keep local flexibilityModularity or local configuration helps.
  5. Sharing is everythingShare the costs and the learning.

Will stressed that the value is not only cash savings. Councils often reinvest what they save into service design and new technology.

Questions to ask in housing

Build together

Like LocalGov Drupal

Are you each paying to build the same thing? Is there something no supplier can build for you alone?

Buy together

Like SELMS

Are you all buying the same thing on your own? Does your supplier have more clout than you?

6. From the Q&A

Vendor lock-in and fragmented systems

Attendees agreed there is strong appetite in housing to talk about shared software. One IT director said suppliers "divide and conquer" the sector and sell lowest-common-denominator products. They offered to explore sharing their organisation's internal integration framework, which connects separate data stores, with other providers. HAILIE is following up on this.

Getting executive buy-in

A participant who had worked on earlier sector data standards said the technical work succeeded but implementation and executive buy-in were hard. Others said providers fear investing years in a shared project only for it to be shut down when staffing or backing runs out. Will said early investors in LocalGov Drupal asked the same questions about payoff and the support burden. A normal supplier relationship is transactional. Collaboration needs trust, open-source licensing, a core team and a fair exchange of money so every party gets what it needs. Long-term viability depends on adoption, financial sustainability and active users.

Competing timelines

How do you manage partners with different deadlines, when executives expect fixed delivery dates? Councils with a hard deadline can build the feature themselves, hire a supplier or pay the core team. Alternatively a "frontier" council builds first and open-sources the work, and the core team runs recap sessions to bring others up to speed and coordinate major releases. Will said the challenge is less herding cats than getting busy people to turn up, so the core team makes decisions and communication transparent for anyone who missed a meeting.

Specialist publishing

The Open Digital Cooperative is seeing demand for specialist publishing, for example from planners producing local plans, where government mandates the output without providing tools. It is a small organisation but open to collaborative conversations.

What this means for housing

  • Start with a need several providers already pay for separately, and get two or three of them to describe it in the same words.
  • Keep governance light at first. A handshake and regular calls is enough for two or three partners.
  • Fund a role, not just a build. Shared work stalls when it is everyone's side project.
  • Consider buying together before building together. Pooled procurement can be faster to set up.

Next steps from the session

  • Legal vehicles: Will will look into legal structures that help open-source projects spread, and share what he finds.
  • Collaboration proposal: Will, HAILIE and a sector data standards colleague will draft a bottom-up proposal for collective collaboration in social housing, designed to help secure executive buy-in.
  • Follow-up session: Will offered a follow-up session on how to manage collaborative software development, with patterns from LocalGov Drupal.
  • Integration framework: HAILIE is exploring whether a member's integration framework can be shared more widely.
